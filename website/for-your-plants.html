Where it works

One pod, everywhere you grow

BioPod doses by pot size, not by plant species. Houseplants on a shelf, ornamentals on a balcony, tomatoes in a patio planter, transplants in a garden bed: if it grows in soil, a pod can feed it. Same pod, same method, same result.

Every place you grow

Five contexts, one pod

The plant changes. The feeding does not.

Houseplants

Pothos to fiddle leaf fig. An indoor pot is a closed system with a few cups of soil, so a gradual organic release suits it better than anything concentrated.

1 pod per 4 to 6 in pot

Balcony and patio pots

Planters, railing boxes, and containers that live outside. Outdoor pots dry faster and drink harder, so check them at the eight week mark rather than ten.

1 pod per 6 in of planter

Herbs and vegetables

Basil on the windowsill, peppers and tomatoes in patio pots. Every input comes from plant and ocean sources, with no synthetic fertilizers anywhere in the blend.

1 pod per plant, by pot size

Ornamentals and flowering plants

Roses, hydrangeas, and container blooms. Potassium supports flowering and root strength, released steadily instead of in a single spike.

1 pod per 6 in of pot

Garden beds and borders

Tuck one pod into the root zone of each young transplant or heavy feeder as you plant it. The nutrients go where the roots are, not across the whole bed.

1 pod per transplant

Small containers

Limited soil, limited margin for error

A garden bed has cubic yards of soil to buffer a mistake. A pot has a few cups. That is what makes container feeding unforgiving, indoors or out.

A container is close to a closed system. There is no rain to flush excess nutrients, no surrounding soil to absorb overflow, and no seasonal reset. A single over-application of concentrated synthetic fertilizer in a six-inch pot can burn roots within hours, because the salts have nowhere to go.

This is exactly the margin BioPod is built for. The dose is pre-measured, the release is gradual, and the nutrients are organic rather than soluble salts, so the failure mode that scares people simply is not on the table.

Read your plant

How to tell if your plant needs more, or less

Signs of underfeeding

  • Slow or no new growth during the growing season
  • Leaves smaller than expected, weak stems
  • Pale or yellowing older leaves while newer leaves stay green

Signs of overfeeding

  • Brown or crispy leaf tips and edges
  • A white salt crust on the soil surface
  • Wilting despite moist soil, rapid yellowing of many leaves

Those overfeeding signs are the classic symptoms of concentrated synthetic salts. With a pre-measured organic pod, underfeeding is the only case you need to watch for, and the fix is simple: add another pod.
